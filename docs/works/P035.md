# P035: The Effect of Images of Michelle Obama's Face on Trick-or-Treaters' Dietary Choices: A Randomized Control Trial

[All works](README.md) · [Machine-readable detail](../../catalog/works/P035.json)

P. M. Aronow, Dean Karlan, and Lauren E. Pinson (2018). The Effect of Images of Michelle Obama's Face on Trick-or-Treaters' Dietary Choices: A Randomized Control Trial. PLOS ONE 13(1):e0189693.

DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0189693](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0189693)

## Overview

This empirical study randomizes a Michelle Obama photograph porch condition and records children's fruit-versus-candy choices across three Halloweens. Its Lean development is limited to finite potential-outcome constructions calibrated to the six released arm frequencies, showing that the porch-condition evidence does not identify the contribution of the nationwide campaign. It does not formalize the empirical estimator, its standard errors, or a causal effect of the campaign in the actual population.

Areas: Causal identification, Experimental design and estimation.

## Corrections and counterexamples

The categorical campaign attribution is not identified by the porch experiment alone: two calibrated local binary potential-outcome worlds have identical observed laws, but the campaign changes no choices in one and enables the positive porch contrast in every year in the other.

## Caveats

- The witnesses are constructed examples with finite latent populations, independent randomized porch assignment, consistency, and no interference; campaign presence is a policy counterfactual rather than individual awareness.
- The Introduction discloses the implemented intervention and proposes campaign reinforcement as a possibility; that rationale is not refuted and is not a separate categorical error.
- Observational equivalence establishes nonidentification, not that the real campaign had zero effect or that reinforcement did not occur; the numerical porch-condition evidence is unchanged.
- The calibration uses complete-case fruit frequencies, including 95 complete cases among 96 participants in the 2012 photograph arm; the illustrative overall effect uses raw enrollment shares and is distinct from the paper’s inverse-probability-weighted estimator.
- P035-T3 is interpretive empirical attribution; the nonidentification theorem addresses the identification implication in P035-T1 and does not determine the campaign’s actual effect.

### P035-T1

A counterexample to the inference behind the first sentence of the Conclusions in the Abstract

**Kind:** `counterexample`

**Location:** Abstract, Conclusions, first sentence, p. 2/6 (PDF p. 2), and the campaign-effect wording in Results and conclusions, p. 5/6 (PDF p. 5); the Introduction on p. 2/6 supplies the research hypothesis and rationale

The paper prints no formal statement here. The Lean statement is the counterexample given in P035-E001 to the identification implication behind the categorical campaign attribution: that the contrast between the two sides of the porch alone establishes an effect of the Let’s Move campaign. The Introduction explicitly proposes reinforcement through a subconscious reminder as a possible explanation; the statement does not formalize that research hypothesis as an error or rule out the proposed pathway. It does not say whether this campaign, Michelle Obama’s initiative to reduce childhood obesity, affected the children in the study. For each year $`y \in \{2012, 2014, 2015\}`$, it defines the hypothetical direct-display model and campaign-mediated model of a child’s choice ($`1`$ for fruit, $`0`$ for candy), $`Y^{\mathrm{D}}_y(z,a;r) = \mathbf{1}\{ r \le t_{y0} + z (t_{y1} - t_{y0}) \}, \qquad Y^{\mathrm{M}}_y(z,a;r) = \mathbf{1}\{ r \le t_{y0} + a z (t_{y1} - t_{y0}) \},`$ where $`z = 1`$ on the Obama side and $`z = 0`$ on the comparison side, and $`a = 1`$ if the campaign is present, as throughout the study. Varying $`a`$ holds the porch condition fixed. The thresholds are $`t_{yz} = L_y p_{yz}`$ for the proportion $`p_{yz}`$ choosing fruit on side $`z`$ in the released data. Here $`L_y`$ is the least common multiple of the reduced denominators of $`p_{y0}`$ and $`p_{y1}`$. The latent value $`r`$ is uniform on $`\{1, \dots, L_y\}`$ and independent of $`z`$ within year. The models grant consistency and no interference: observed choice is the potential choice at the assigned side, and it does not depend on other children’s assignments. These conditions are not established for the actual trials by this construction. The statement asserts that $`Y^{\mathrm{D}}_y(z,1;r) = Y^{\mathrm{M}}_y(z,1;r)`$ for every $`y`$, $`z`$ and $`r`$. Both models reproduce the six proportions, and with them the positive contrast $`p_{y1} - p_{y0} = (t_{y1} - t_{y0})/L_y`$ between the two sides. This calibration does not equate sample frequencies with unknown population probabilities. The effect of the campaign, the change from $`a=0`$ to $`a=1`$ at fixed $`z`$ and $`r`$, is $`0`$ for every child in the direct-display model and has mean $`p_{y1} - p_{y0}`$ on the Obama side in the campaign-mediated model; on the comparison side it is $`0`$ in both models. The distribution of year, side and choice does not determine the mean effect of the campaign, by year or overall, among models that reproduce the six proportions. Years form a type with three elements; $`z`$, $`a`$ and the choice are Boolean; $`r`$ is a natural number; means are finite sums of rational numbers; and the counts from the released data that give the six proportions are entered as constants. The Lean statement is broader than the counterexample in that the pointwise identities above hold for every natural number $`r`$, not only for $`r \in \{1, \dots, L_y\}`$. The two models also give the same weighted sums for year, side and choice under arbitrary rational weights on $`(y, z, r)`$, which need not be probabilities. Its overall mean effect weights years and sides by the raw shares of enrolment, not by the inverse probability weights of the paper’s pooled estimate.

### P035-T2

Abstract, Conclusions, first sentence, as corrected

**Kind:** `corrected_statement`

**Location:** p. 2/6 (PDF p. 2); the phrase “the treatment effect of her campaign” on p. 5/6 (PDF p. 5)

The Lean statement covers the mathematical content of the following claim about Michelle Obama’s initiative, the Let’s Move campaign: “Campaign reinforcement is a possible explanation; the experiment does not establish that the initiative or its health message caused the difference.” It asserts that there exist two models of the children’s potential choices such that year, side and choice have the same distribution in both models. Each model reproduces the six proportions of children choosing fruit in the released data, one for each year and side of the porch. In the first model the campaign changes no child’s choice. In the second its mean effect on the Obama side is positive in each year and equals the whole contrast between the proportions on the two sides. The statement adds that this distribution does not determine the mean effect of the campaign on the Obama side among models that reproduce the six proportions. A model gives a binary choice for each year, side, state of the campaign (present or absent) and latent value $`r`$, where $`r`$ is uniform on a finite set and independent of the side. The campaign is thus a policy that is present or absent for all children; it is not a child’s awareness of the campaign or a separate effect of its health message. The Lean statement also requires that the second model give each child the same choice on both sides when the campaign is absent, an additional restriction on the second model. It does not estimate the empirical effect of the porch condition or establish a separate effect of the campaign’s health message.

### P035-T3

Abstract, Conclusions, first sentence, as printed

**Kind:** `result_touched_by_finding`

**Location:** Abstract, Conclusions, first sentence, p. 2/6 (PDF p. 2), and the campaign-effect wording in Results and conclusions, p. 5/6 (PDF p. 5); read with the Introduction’s hypothesis and rationale on p. 2/6

This sentence is an empirical attribution rather than a mathematical statement. It reads “Michelle Obama’s initiative to reduce childhood obesity has influenced children’s dietary preferences”. The Introduction supplies an explicit rationale: a subconscious reminder may reinforce the campaign’s message, while evaluation of the entire nationwide campaign is said to be impossible. That research hypothesis need not be read as a separately established mechanism. The paper does not define a campaign-effect parameter or impose a restriction that identifies some campaign contribution from the porch-condition contrast. The identification implication behind the categorical attribution in the abstract and the campaign-effect wording on p. 5/6 is formalized separately as the counterexample given in P035-E001. It shows what the experiment alone does not determine; it neither refutes the proposed reminder pathway nor settles whether the initiative in fact influenced these children.
